Abstract
The development of large language models (LLMs) poses new chal-
lenges in data center network topology design. To assist in exploring
topology design, we propose ATOP, an Automated Topology Op-
timization Pipeline, which models network topology as a set of
hyperparameters, enabling the discovery of potential topologies.
With various optimization algorithms and customizable optimiza-
tion objectives, ATOP achieves automated topology optimization
on a scale of tens of thousands of GPUs. We apply ATOP on net-
work topologies for 256, 1024, 4096, and 16384 GPUs, optimizing
performance under LLMs training traffic patterns, collective com-
munication performance, fault tolerance, and network cost. We
also evaluate ATOP in different scenarios: building, optimizing, and
expanding a data center. From ATOP’s results, we discover a new
topology — ZCube, which reaches the highest cost-effectiveness
across various GPU scales. Simulation results show that ZCube,
compared to the previous state-of-the-art topologies, including Rail-
optimized Fat-tree (ROFT), Rail-only, and HPN, improves end-to-
end LLM training speed by 3% to 7% and reduces network hardware
costs by 26% to 46%. We also construct ZCube on a real-world
testbed. Results show that ZCube reduces hardware costs by 25%
compared to Rail-Optimized Topology while maintaining the same
all-reduce and all-to-all performance.

Introduction
With the emergence of generative AI, the exponential growth of
model sizes has led to the construction of increasingly large and
expensive training clusters. For example, ByteDance has built a
cluster of over 10k GPUs [29]. Meta built a cluster of 24k GPUs for
Llama-3 training [23]. X.AI has also invested at least $3 billion [18]
in AI infrastructure.
Designing inter-server topologies for large clusters requires op-
timizing multiple objectives, and the most important ones include
performance, fault tolerance, and cost. There are complex trade-offs
among these objectives, and it is virtually impossible to achieve
optimality in all dimensions. Therefore, it is practical to seek cost-
effective topologies–those that offer the best performance within
a given budget or the lowest cost for a given performance level,
i.e., Pareto-optimal [9] topologies.
Current topology design processes rely on selecting topologies
from the HPC and DCN literature [23, 29]. However, we find that
well-known topologies fall short of Pareto-optimality. We con-
duct extensive experiments showing that Rail-optimized Fat-tree
(ROFT) [39], Rail-only [51], and HPN 7.0 [44] are not Pareto-optimal.

The results reveal that many alternative designs outperform these

Topology design is also not a “one-time” deal. There are at least
three operational scenarios that necessitate design processes: (1)
Initial construction, where operators architect new data centers
to meet specific requirements, such as new model architectures
(e.g., Mixture-of-Experts models [33]) and new networking hard-
ware (e.g., next generation switching chips [22]); (2) Incremental
adjustments, e.g., re-cabling to mitigate bottlenecks or reduce oper-
ational costs; and (3) Expansion, e.g., scaling from 1k to 4k GPUs
while preserving fault tolerance. Automating the design process is
thus a practical and worthwhile pursuit.
Therefore, cluster topology design is a practical and challeng-
ing task involving multiple objectives: end-to-end performance
of target workloads, fault tolerance, cost constraints, hardware
availability, etc. There are currently two main design approaches:
manual and automated. Manual design by human experts is the
most common approach, but it suffers from three issues.
• The topology design space for large-scale clusters is vast, mak-
ing it difficult for manual design to efficiently explore the topol-
ogy space.
• Human experts are biased towards aesthetically pleasing topolo-
gies and may overlook non-intuitive topologies with better
properties.
• Manual design also struggles to account for multiple objectives
simultaneously.
Thus, both industry and academia are investigating automated
topology design methods. The naïve method is to model topolo-
gies using adjacency matrices, but this introduces an enormous
search space, which is impractical to search. The seminal work,
Condor [48], addresses this issue by introducing a domain-specific
language (DSL) of topology constraints and models the topology
design as a constraint satisfiability problem. However, it still re-
lies on human experts to express their intuition using the DSL.
Although the DSL makes it easy for experts to describe hardware
constraints (e.g., per switch port count) and failure tolerance prop-
erties (e.g., maintain full connectivity in single-switch failures), it
is very difficult and unintuitive to express application-level, end-to-
end performance optimization goals (e.g., minimizing LLM training
iteration time) using a constraint language.
Thus, we ask the question: can we combine human intuitions
and automated exploration in cluster topology design with multiple
objectives? To this end, we propose ATOP, an Automated Topology
Optimization Pipeline. Specifically, ATOP addresses the limitations
of existing topology design methods from three key aspects.
• Intuition-driven Modeling: We study all the popular DCN
and HPC topologies, and distill the expert intuitions behind
each design. We then formalize the intuitions into 11 types
of searchable hyperparameters, which construct a reasonably
large topology space. ATOP’s modeling approach covers most
known existing topologies, such as CLOS [13, 23, 29], Fat-
Tree [2], ROFT [39], Rail-only [51], HPN [44], BCube [25],
DCell [26], HyperX [1], Torus [30], Dragonfly [32], etc. The
topologies generated by ATOP are not limited to variants or
combinations of these existing designs but are capable of pro-
ducing new asymmetric topologies, providing a high degree of
freedom in the search space while reasonably constraining its
scope.
• Multi-Objective Optimization via Guided Simulation: ATOP
combines the NSGA-II evolutionary algorithm [15] with a high-
performance, high-fidelity simulator to efficiently explore topol-
ogy designs. The optimizer iteratively generates candidate topolo-
gies using non-dominated sorting and crowding distance met-
rics, while the simulator uses realistic workloads to evalu-
ate each candidate against user-defined objectives—including
throughput, fault tolerance, cost, and network diameter.
• Efficiency and Scalability: ATOP improves efficiency and scal-
ability in three aspects: Firstly, our topology modeling approach
reasonably reduces the search space. Secondly, we improve the
scalability of Astra-Sim [54] with a flow-level simulator which
emphasizes fidelity. This simulator can efficiently evaluate the
performance of large-scale topologies with real LLM training
workloads. Finally, we design a 2-stage evaluation method to
significantly reduce the number of evaluations: the first stage
employs representative fragments of model training workloads,
producing a Pareto-optimal set of topologies. The second stage
runs a complete LLM training workload for each of the topolo-
gies to obtain the final results. With these techniques, ATOP
is able to discover new superior topologies within 3 days on a
single CPU server for a cluster with 16384 GPUs.
We evaluate ATOP in the above three design scenarios (§4). For
example, in the design of initial construction, we use ATOP to
optimize topology for varying cluster sizes (from 256 to 16384).
From the results, we are surprised to discover that, given practical
performance objectives (LLM training iteration time, real network
cost models, and physical constraints of current hardware), the
most cost-effective topologies among all the topologies we have
searched in the three scenarios are similar, and, to the best of our
knowledge, have not been disclosed. We then formalize this new
topology, and name it ZCube. We verify the performance of ZCube
through packet-level simulation, theoretical analysis, and building
a real testbed with 16 GPUs and 32×200GbE ports. We run the
nccl-tests [40], and find that ZCube reduces hardware costs by
25% compared to non-block ROFT while maintaining the same
all-reduce and all-to-all performance.
The main contributions of our work are as follows.
• We summarize the expert insight of most existing DCN and HPC
topologies, and propose a new abstract model with 11 types of
searchable hyperparameters, thereby obtaining a reasonably
sized search space that includes all popular topologies.
• We propose ATOP, a novel topology design system. It achieves
multi-objective optimization with a high-performance, high-
fidelity simulator guided by an evolutionary algorithm. Users
can freely define objectives and constraints for ATOP to adapt
to different scenarios.
• We develop a 2-stage evaluation method that can reduce the
number of full-scale model training simulations necessary for
ATOP’s topology optimization by 95 %. ATOP is able to discover
new superior topologies within 3 days on a single CPU server
for a cluster with 16384 GPUs.

Background and Motivation
In this section, we introduce the background of large model training,
large-scale AI cluster topologies, design methods, and motivate the
design of ATOP.
LLM Training Traffic Patterns. Distributed training techniques
parallelize the LLM training workload, each generating distinct
communication patterns. Understanding these patterns is crucial
for designing efficient cluster topologies. To study these communi-
cation patterns, we record traffic traces from large-scale training
runs on a production cluster with 4k GPUs. We also use the AICB
workload generator in SimAI [3] and conduct interviews with LLM
researchers and practitioners. We focus only on the inter-server
traffic; thus we exclude tensor parallelism (TP).
• Data parallelism (DP) partitions training data across GPUs.
Each GPU computes gradients on its local partition, which are
then aggregated to update global model parameters, generating
all-gather (AG) and reduce-scatter (RS) collective communica-
tion [35], with message size from 10 to 100 MB.
• Pipeline parallelism (PP) splits the model across devices,
with each GPU handling a layer or set of layers. Inputs pass
forward through this pipeline, with each GPU performing its
computation and passing activations to the next GPU. Gradients
are passed backward through the same pipeline, generating
point-to-point (P2P) communication [35], with message size
1-10 MB.
Figure 2: GPT-3 training timeline on rank 0 of classical in-
terleaved 1F1B schedule, excluding TP communication as it
typically occurs on the intra-server network.
• Expert parallelism (EP), used in mixture-of-expert (MoE)
models, generates all-to-all traffic among GPUs [45]. We learn
from LLM researchers and training frameworks that EP traffic
may co-exist with DP traffic but rarely with PP traffic [45, 49,
53, 55, 57].
• Co-existence. By analyzing actual traffic traces (e.g., Fig. 2),
we find that, during the actual training, five types of traffic
periods exist: DP-only, PP-only, EP-only, mixed DP-PP, and
mixed DP-EP.
We find that these traffic share a common characteristic: cross-
server communication typically occurs between GPUs with the
same index (e.g., from GPU_2 on Server_1 to GPU_2 on Server_64).
This remains true for all-to-all communication because PXN in
NCCL first forwards traffic to the same-index GPU using NVLink
and then to the destination server [42]. This traffic characteristic is
called rail-aligned [51]. In contrast, inter-rail traffic refers to inter-
server communication that occurs between GPUs with different
indexes. We observe that rail-aligned traffic also traverses spine
switches in ROFT because the communicating servers may belong
to different pods, for example, PP traffic and collective communica-
tion generated by large DP group typically. During one month of
monitoring in a production GPU cluster with ROFT topology, we
observe that 35% of rail-aligned traffic traverses spine switches.
With the above analysis, we prepare representative workloads
for each traffic period to use in the 2-stage evaluation mechanism
of ATOP and ensure that all traffic is rail-aligned and there is no
inter-rail traffic.
Large-Scale GPUs Interconnection Topology. Training LLMs
requires thousands of GPUs (1k ∼10k) [35, 45], making efficient
GPU interconnection critical. Intra-server GPU connections use
NVLink or PCIe, while inter-server connections rely on Ethernet,
Infiniband (IB), or RDMA over Converged Ethernet (RoCE). As intra-
server networks are usually optimized by manufacturers, we focus
on inter-server network topologies. Currently, the most commonly
used topologies in practice include CLOS and ROFT [23, 29, 39].
ROFT connects GPUs with the same index in different servers via
a single ToR switch (e.g., the GPU_0 of all the servers in the same
pod are connected). Rail-only constructs a Rail-interconnection for
GPUs with the same index on each server, eliminating the inter-
rail interconnection. Alibaba’s HPN [44] uses dual-port NICs to
build a dual-port designs for ROFT (dual-plane architecture). In
Appendix K, we provide diagrams illustrating ROFT, Rail-only, and
HPN topologies built with 51.2 Tbps switches for 16k GPU clusters.
Current topologies make different trade-offs between perfor-
mance, cost, and fault tolerance.
• Performance drives topology design toward full bisection band-
width. ROFT [23, 29, 39], Rail-only, and HPN [44] all achieve this
theoretical maximum. However, theoretical performance does

network hardware costs by 10% compared to Rail-only due to

• Fault tolerance is another key concern of topology design. ROFT
and Rail-only exhibit poor fault tolerance: a single ToR fail-
ure affects many GPUs, forcing them to use PXN for traffic
forwarding through intra-server interconnects. This creates
intra-server bandwidth competition and impacts other parallel
strategies. Fig. 3(b) shows that one ToR failure in a 4k GPU clus-
ter degrades LLM training performance by 46.9% in ROFT and
46.2% in Rail-only. HPN addresses this vulnerability through
its dual-plane architecture but at increased cost.
The complex trade-off between performance, cost, and fault
tolerance motivates us to develop an automated topology design
tool that identifies the Pareto-optimal topologies and discovers new
topologies that combine high cost-effectiveness with strong fault
tolerance.
Existing Topology Design Methods. There are currently two
main approaches for topology design: manual and automated. For
the manual method, designers often rely on expert knowledge
to select a highly symmetric topology. This focus on symmetry
can overlook potentially high-performance asymmetric topologies.
Additionally, designing a topology that meets multiple performance
criteria is challenging, such as when ROFT excels in all-reduce but
falls short in cost and fault tolerance.
For the automated method, one naïve approach involves using ad-
jacency matrix-based topology modeling for exhaustive search [14],
but this results in an infeasibly large search space. For 𝑁GPUs,
the space grows as 𝑂(2𝑁2), making it impractical to quickly find
superior designs. For instance, we implement a program for search-
ing 16k GPU topologies using an adjacency matrix, which takes
10 hours without producing a connected topology, as hundreds
of millions of parameters are required to represent the topology.
Transforming a ROFT topology into Dragonfly requires modifying
hundreds of millions of parameters.
Previous seminal work Condor [48] has partially addressed the
problem of automated design. Schlinker et al. developed a topology
description language and introduced constraint solving to this area.
However, since describing topology features that benefit application
performance still relies on human intuition, this method cannot
directly reason about end-to-end application performance.
Thus, we conclude that existing approaches are reaching their
limits as the demands of modern workloads grow in complexity
and scale. Naïve automated approaches lack the domain insights
to effectively constrain the search, while manual design relies on

In this work, we propose to combine human insight with auto-

expert design intuitions of past topologies into a unified set of
searchable hyperparameters, which concisely cover a rich space
of topologies, and 2) searching this hyperparameter space with a
scalable multi-objective evolutionary algorithm guided by high-
fidelity simulations. This enables the principled discovery of novel
high-performance topologies that are flexibly optimized for the
unique requirements of each specific workload and cluster, cover-
ing metrics like throughput, cost, and reliability.

Design of ATOP
We propose ATOP, an Automated Topology Optimization Pipeline,
to address the challenges in §1. ATOP consists of three main com-
ponents: Topology Hyperparameterization Modeling (§3.2), which
defines the topology search space; Topology Optimizer (§3.3), which
searches and optimizes topologies; and Topology Evaluator (§3.4),
which evaluates the given topology. An overview of ATOP is shown
in Fig. 4.
3.1
Insights from Existing Topologies
Existing topologies leverage expert knowledge to solve specific
problems. Fat-Tree improves aggregation bandwidth in tree topolo-
gies, ROFT optimizes inter-server all-reduce in GPU clusters, and
HPN enhances ROFT’s fault tolerance with a dual-ToR architecture.
BCube accelerates one-to-many traffic in modular data centers,
while Dragonfly reduces network diameter and cost with low-radix
switches. These topologies incorporate hierarchical structures and
symmetry, derived from expert experience. We aim to develop a
design approach that balances these expert insights with the flex-
ibility of the topology search space to efficiently discover high-
performance, deployable topologies that may feature certain asym-
metric structures.
To this end, we summarize 11 types of hyperparameters from
expert intuitions, constructing a searchable topology space that

Insight-driven Topology Modeling
ATOP’s modeling design is based on an analysis of existing DCN
topologies. These topologies typically feature layered structures,
symmetry (e.g., HPN, Fat-Tree, BCube), and may include intra-
layer connections (e.g., Dragonfly, HyperX, DCell). Thus, ATOP’s
topology modeling includes two main components: “Node Layering
and Inter-layer Connections” and “Intra-layer Connections”. Tab. 1
summarizes the notation used in this section.

3.3
Topology Optimizer
Optimization Objectives. Optimization objectives are inputs to
ATOP and can be customized based on the user’s requirements.
Our optimization objectives include job completion time (JCT) for
representative traffic in LLM training, simulated with a network
simulator, ForestColl all-gather [58], average path length under
single-switch failure, and network cost. Detailed implementations
are provided in §4.1.
Topology Optimizer. The optimizer samples the hyperparame-
ter space, generates a topology for evaluation, and receives per-
formance feedback to guide subsequent sampling. ATOP’s opti-
mizer uses NSGA-II as the optimization algorithm due to its per-
formance and efficiency [31, 34, 47]. To improve search efficiency,
we also implement parallel optimization, allowing simultaneous
multi-topology evaluations with multiple processors, resulting in
linear efficiency gains. We also validate ATOP’s convergence and
efficiency in Appendix D and compare NSGA-II with other opti-
mization algorithms in Appendix J.
3.4
Topology Evaluator
Flow-level Simulator. Efficient evaluation of large-scale topolo-
gies is challenging. Packet-level simulators like NS-3 [46] can assess
small-scale topologies within minutes but become impractical for
thousands of GPUs due to excessive time and memory demands [24].
To address this, we implement a flow-level network simulator that
models communication flows between GPUs. The simulator ap-
plies the max-min fairness bandwidth allocation [36] algorithm for
each flow. For flow collisions, we only use the max-min fairness
algorithm to allocate bandwidth and don’t use additional penalty
functions. For congestion modeling and switch delay, we use the
approach from SimGrid [8] for improved accuracy. The delay of
each link can be configured in the topology description file. We
validate the accuracy of the simulator by comparing its results with
NS-3 under the same configurations. Results (Tab. 4 in Appendix I)
show the average job completion time error between the two sim-
ulators is 1.5 % across different scales and topologies in the LLMs
training scenario.
2-stage Evaluation. We design a 2-stage evaluation method to
reduce the number of time-consuming end-to-end large model train-
ing simulations. In the first stage, we evaluate representative frag-
ments of model training workloads, fault tolerance, and network
cost as optimization objectives. Due to multi-objective optimiza-
tion, ATOP can produce a set of Pareto-optimal topologies (defined
in Appendix C) as the first-stage results. In the second stage, we
evaluate the end-to-end training times for these topologies using
Astra-Sim 2.0 [54] with our flow-level network simulator as the
backend and SimAI [3] for workload generation. Finally, we obtain
end-to-end training results for all Pareto-optimal topologies.
Collective Communication Performance. Although the flow-
level simulator evaluates topologies under LLM traffic, existing
collective communication algorithms (e.g., Ring [42] and RHD [50])
may not be optimal for a given topology. Therefore, ATOP im-
plements ForestColl’s [58] algorithm to compute the theoretical
minimum all-gather time. ForestColl’s algorithm consists of two
parts: part 1 computes the theoretical lower bound on the comple-
tion time of an optimal collective algorithm for a given topology,
while part 2 derives the actual collective algorithm. While Forest-
Coll can quickly compute the theoretical optimal completion time
(part 1), obtaining the concrete collective algorithm and routing
(part 2) is time-consuming. Therefore, we only use part 1 to eval-
uate the topology, serving as one of the optimization objectives
during the search process. This evaluation does not use the sim-
ulator but relies on a theoretical approach to assess performance
under optimal algorithms and ideal load-balancing.

4
Implementation & Evaluation of ATOP
In this section, we discuss the general configurations of ATOP, three
use cases, as well as ATOP’s search results.
4.1
Methodology & Setup
TestBed. We evaluate ATOP on a single server with 256 AMD EPYC
7Y83 CPU cores and 1 TB of memory.
Search Space. We set 𝐿max to 4 as topologies with more layers may
have a larger network diameter, resulting in higher latency. We set
𝐷max to 4 since most topologies do not exceed four dimensions in
intra-layer interconnections.
Optimizer and Objectives. We use NSGA-II for hyperparameter
optimization, with 4 types and 14 objectives:
(1) Job Completion Time (JCT) for training traffic patterns (DP-
only, PP-only, EP-only, Mixed DP-PP, Mixed DP-EP) using a flow-
level network simulator. For non-MoE models, we consider different
common parallel strategies. We set TP=8 for the NVIDIA H100
server, which interconnects 8 GPUs with NVLink, and consider PP
values of 4, 8, and 16. DP is the number of GPUs divided by TP
and PP, using RHD [50] collective communication algorithm. These

traffic patterns generate 9 objectives (3 parallel strategies × 3 traffic
patterns). For MoE models, we consider 256 experts with EP=256,
DP is the number of GPUs divided by EP, yielding 2 objectives
(EP-only, Mixed DP-EP). In total, the above traffic patterns generate

(2) For ForestColl all-gather, we set the data volume to 1 GB,
which is typical for DP in large model training.
(3) For fault tolerance, we use average path length under single-
switch failure, as in Equation 2, to estimate topology performance
during failures.
(4) For topology cost estimation, we consider switches, cables
(copper, fiber cables), optical modules, and NICs. Prices are sourced
from FS [19] and Colfax Direct [16]. We use switches with dif-
ferent throughputs: Broadcom N8550-40CD (8 Tbps), N9550-32D
(12.8 Tbps), N9550-64D (25.6 Tbps), and N9600-64OD (51.2 Tbps).
NICs include NVIDIA ConnectX-6 and ConnectX-7, and optical
modules are NVIDIA OSFP Optical Modules series based on band-
width. Cable selection depends on distance: Direct Attach Cable
(DAC) for short-range (≤3 meters), optical modules and MTP
jumpers (fiber cables) for long-range. For NIC-to-first-layer switch
interconnections, if a rail-optimized method is used (i.e., NICs on
the same server connect to different switches), we assume 15-meter
cables with MTP jumpers and optical modules. If all NICs on the
same server connect to the same ToR, we use 3-meter DACs. For
adjacent-layer switches, we use 30-meter MTP jumpers, and for
cross-layer (e.g., layer-1 to layer-3) and intra-layer connections, we
use 50-meter MTP jumpers.
Number of Topology Searches and Search Duration. Despite a
reduced search space, evaluating all topologies remains infeasible.
We limit the optimizer to explore up to 105 topologies. ATOP com-
pletes the search and 2-stage evaluation in 6.5 hours for 256 GPUs,
10.6 hours for 1k GPUs, 25.4 hours for 4k GPUs, and 71.2 hours for
16k GPUs, with 80% of the time spent on topology evaluation. We
have already parallelized the search and performed the search pro-
cess with 256 CPU cores. These times are reasonable compared to
the months required for data center deployment. Moreover, metrics
like HyperVolume [60] can also serve as stopping criteria. We also
discuss ATOP’s convergence in Fig. 16 of Appendix D. The results
demonstrate that ATOP shows convergence across three metrics.
Second Stage Evaluation Setup. In the second evaluation stage,
we conduct end-to-end large model training simulations for Pareto-
optimal topologies using Astra-Sim 2.0. We evaluate topologies
for GPT-3-22B training on 256 GPUs and GPT-3-175B training on
1k, 4k, and 16k GPUs. For parallel strategies, we adopt common
configurations [4, 11, 35, 56]: TP=8, PP=8, and DP as the number
of GPUs divided by TP and PP, with the interleaved 1F1B strategy.
We use intra-server interconnects for TP communication and inter-
server interconnects for DP and PP. We arrange the parallel strategy
consistent with Megatron-LM [35], which makes efforts to put a set
of tightly-coupled GPUs more closely together to minimize switch
hops. We record the computation time for each model’s operator
on the NVIDIA H100 GPU. Detailed model parameters and training
configurations are provided in Tab. 8 in the Appendix. The 2-stage
evaluation method avoids 105 full-scale model training simulations,
reducing evaluation to approximately 5000 (5 %) Pareto-optimal
topologies and achieving a 20-fold speedup.
Topology Evaluator and Simulation Configuration. In the flow-
level network simulator, the baseline inter-server link bandwidth
is set to 200 Gbps, adjustable via the bandwidth factor. Link de-
lay is 5 µs, with the total latency of each flow dependent on hop
count. For 8-GPU NVIDIA H100 servers, intra-server GPUs are in-
terconnected via NVLink and NVSwitch with 900 GB/s bidirectional
bandwidth and 1 µs latency, consistent with the NVIDIA DGX H100
server [39] specifications. During simulation evaluation, PXN is
enabled and routes traffic via NVLink to minimize hop count when
beneficial. ECMP [28] is employed for routing and load balancing,
chosen for its deployability as the de-facto standard in commercial
RoCE switches [20–22]. Topologies performing well under ideal
load-balancing but poorly under ECMP are difficult to deploy di-
rectly using commercial RoCE switches. Considering deployability
and simulation efficiency, we use ECMP for evaluation. For fault
tolerance, intra-server interconnection is considered, and PXN is
also enabled. We treat each 8-GPU server as having point-to-point
connections among GPUs, with a communication path length of 1.
Since Pareto-optimal topologies are selected from all generated by
ATOP, a topology that disconnects after a ToR switch failure but
excels in other metrics may still be chosen, ensuring ATOP does
not generate only multi-ToR topologies.
4.2
Use Cases of ATOP
Next, we show that ATOP is useful in the three operational scenarios
in §1.
4.2.1
Use Case 1: Designing a New Data Center.
Setup. In designing a new data center, ATOP is employed to ex-
plore topologies at various scales: 256, 1024, 4096, and 16384 GPUs.
Switch throughput limits are set to 6.4 Tbps for 256 GPUs, 12.8 Tbps
for 1024 GPUs, 25.6 Tbps for 4096 GPUs, and 51.2 Tbps for 16384
GPUs. For NICs, we consider configurations with multiple NICs per
GPU, including multi-port NICs, with each GPU accommodating
up to 8 NIC ports, each with at least 200 Gbps bandwidth. The max-
imum outbound bandwidth per GPU is capped at 1.6 Tbps. A GPU
with 400 Gbps outbound bandwidth can utilize either a single-port
400 Gbps NIC or a dual-port 2 × 200 Gbps NIC. ATOP can freely
explore NIC port counts and bandwidth configurations for each
GPU.
Results. Fig. 1 visualizes the search results for data centers at var-
ious scales. Since the first-stage evaluation uses 14 optimization
objectives, we only show end-to-end LLM training evaluation re-
sults of Pareto-optimal topologies. Each point represents a topology.
ATOP identifies the Pareto frontier for training time and cost, with
Pareto-optimal topologies positioned near this frontier. A compar-
ison of 100,000 ATOP-discovered topologies and Pareto-optimal
topologies for the 4k-GPU scale is shown in Fig. 6. Results show that
ATOP captures topologies on the performance-cost Pareto frontier,
validating the feasibility of using representative traffic patterns
during training to evaluate topologies.
From these results, we observe the following pattern: perfor-
mance improves with increased cost within a range, but further cost
escalation yields diminishing returns once a performance threshold
is reached.
We identify three key topologies for each GPU scale: the most
cost-effective, best-performance, and budget-friendly topologies.

4.2.2
Use Case 2: Adjusting an Existing Data Center.
Setup. In this scenario, we use ATOP to optimize the topology of a
4k GPU data center. The initial topology is a 3-layer non-blocking
ROFT architecture. We aim to improve performance while reducing
network costs by re-wiring without purchasing additional switches
and NICs. Modifications to optical modules and cables are allowed.
The switch throughput is 25.6 Tbps, the number of switches is 320,
and each GPU can use a dual-port NIC. These constraints strictly
limit the topology search space.
Results. Fig. 7(a) presents the ATOP search results in this scenario.
We discover that ZCube is both the best-performance and cost-
effective topology within this constrained search space. The strict
limitations on the number of switches and throughput make con-
structing topologies with higher performance difficult. ATOP also
identifies the Pareto frontier of training time and network cost in
this scenario. Topologies on the Pareto frontier represent potential
options from which users can select based on their budget.

4.3
Discussion
ATOP is a useful tool for data center architects and is not limited
to the above three applications. Evolving hardware configurations,
shifting traffic patterns, and dynamic user requirements often ne-
cessitate the discovery of novel topologies. By adjusting its inputs,
ATOP can dynamically adapt to these changes and efficiently iden-
tify optimized topologies that meet updated constraints. For exam-
ple, in Appendix A& B, we show that ATOP can also produce new
and high-quality topologies for a multi-tenant data center and a
heterogeneous data center, respectively.
From the results of ATOP, we observe that the topologies at the
inflection point (cost-effective) share similar architectures. Most
of them have two or three layers of switches, with connections
between GPUs and switches at each layer, as well as connections
between the switches. Both users and we found this topology to be
of significant interest and proceeded with small-scale deployments
(§6.2). We summarize and define this type of topology as ZCube.
A formal description and analysis of ZCube across different GPU
scales are provided in §5 and §6.

ZCube(𝑛,𝑘+ 1) is equipped with (𝑘+ 1) NIC ports numbered from

for each GPU. For instance, when constructing ZCube(𝑛, 2), each
GPU only needs to be equipped with a 400 Gbps NIC, which is then
divided into 2 × 200 Gbps ports and connected to different switches,
or alternatively, using two separate 200 Gbps NICs.
The connections between switches and GPUs in ZCube(𝑛,𝑘+ 1)
can be constructed as follows. We number the 𝑛ZCube(𝑛,𝑘) from
0 to 𝑛−1 and the GPUs in each ZCube(𝑛,𝑘) from 0 to 𝑛𝑘−1. We
then connect the level-𝑘NIC of the 𝑖-th GPU (𝑖∈[0,𝑛𝑘−1]) in the
𝑗-th ZCube(𝑛,𝑘) (𝑗∈[0,𝑛−1]) to the 𝑗-th port of the 𝑖-th level-𝑘
switch.
For the connections between switches, each level-(𝑘−1) switch
connects to 𝑛level-𝑘switches. The 𝑖-th level-(𝑘−1) switch (𝑖∈
[0,𝑛𝑘−1 −1]) in the 𝑗-th ZCube(𝑛,𝑘) (𝑗∈[0,𝑛−1]) connects to
𝑛level-𝑘switches, represented as the 𝑚-th switch (𝑚∈[𝑖· 𝑛, (𝑖+
1) · 𝑛−1]) in level-𝑘, as illustrated in Fig. 8(a). We also present a
schematic of ZCube(2, 3) in Fig. 8(b).
In ZCube(𝑛,𝑘), level-0 and level-(𝑘−1) switches require 2𝑛
ports, while switches at the intermediate levels require 3𝑛ports,
highlighting the asymmetry of ZCube. In traditional topologies
such as Fat-Tree, BCube, and Dragonfly, switches typically use the
same number of ports, reflecting a preference for symmetry. This
focus on symmetry may lead designers to overlook asymmetrical
yet high-performance topologies. Using ATOP can help designers
explore asymmetrical topologies.

Evaluation of ZCube
We evaluate the ZCube’s performance and compare it with various
topologies at different scales. In §6.1, to evaluate the performance
of these topologies under packet-spraying [17] load balancing, we
adopt a packet-level simulator (htsim [7]) as the backend of Astra-
Sim 2.0 [54]. We measure the end-to-end iteration time for training
GPT-3 175B and MoE-GPT models. We also evaluate the theoretical
ForestColl all-gather performance of the topologies in §6.1, which
shows these topologies have identical theoretical ForestColl all-
gather performance. In §6.2, we deploy real-world ZCube(4, 2) and
ROFT on 4 servers with 16 H800 GPUs and test the performance of
NCCL.
6.1
End-to-end LLMs Training Performance
We evaluate the training iteration time of LLMs across various
topologies at scales of 1024, 4096, and 16384 GPUs (with topology
configurations detailed in Appendix L). All topologies share the
same server configuration: each server has 8 H100 GPUs connected
via 900 GB/s NVLink. Each server is equipped with 8 NICs, provid-
ing a total outbound bandwidth of 3.2 Tbps. We deploy two models:
GPT-3 175B and MoE-GPT. For GPT-3 175B training, the parallel
strategy is consistent with §4.1. For MoE-GPT, we follow the con-
figuration in DeepSpeed-MoE [45]. We set 256 experts with EP=256.
The DP of the MoE layer is the number of GPUs divided by the
EP. For non-MoE layers, we set TP=8, and the DP is the number of
GPUs divided by the TP. Other configurations remain consistent
with GPT-3 175B training.
In all evaluations, we enable PXN and ensure that all traffic
is rail-aligned. In the packet-level network simulator—htsim [7],
we integrate Broadcom’s Tomahawk5 RoCE switch model and en-
able the Dynamic Load Balancing (DLB) feature, a packet-spraying
mechanism that dynamically selects a port for each packet based
on factors such as port status, queue depth, and latency. In Appen-
dix H, we compare the collective communication results between
the simulator and the real-world testbed in §6.2, the results show
an average relative error of only 5%.
As shown in Fig. 9, ZCube demonstrates the best performance
in training both non-MoE and MoE models. Compared to ROFT,
Rail-only, and HPN, ZCube improves end-to-end LLM training time
by 3% to 7% and reduces network hardware costs by 26% to 46%.
This acceleration comes from ZCube’s low-diameter property. For
example, Fig. 10 shows the flow completion time CDF of PP traffic.
In ROFT, Rail-only, and HPN, PP traffic typically requires 3 or even
5 hops, while in ZCube it only requires 2 hops. In Appendix K,
we present the topology diagrams of ROFT, Rail-only, HPN, and
ZCube under a 16384 GPU scale, highlighting the cost-effectiveness
of ZCube.
6.2
Building and Testing a Real ZCube
We deploy the ZCube topology in a real-world setting and conduct
NCCL-based collective communication tests (without PP), confirm-
ing its superior cost-effectiveness.
Our testbed consists of 4 servers and 8 Mellanox QM9790 In-
finiteBand switches [37]. Each server has 8 NVIDIA H800 GPUs.
GPUs in the same server are interconnected with 200 GB/s NVLink.
We use 16 NVIDIA ConnectX-7 single-port 400GbE NICs [38] to
build the ROFT topology and 16 ConnectX-7 dual-port 2×200GbE
NICs [38] for the ZCube topology, as shown in Fig. 11. To avoid
NIC bandwidth contention between GPUs during testing, we only
use 4 GPUs per server, totaling 16 GPUs for the collective com-
munication tests. In this configuration, each GPU in both ZCube
and ROFT has 400 Gbps of outbound bandwidth, and each server
has a total outbound bandwidth of 1.6 Tbps. For routing and load
balancing, we use hand-crafted static optimal routing to eliminate
flow collisions.
We evaluate their performance using NCCL 2.21.5 [42] and nccl-
tests [40]. We use the default collective communication algorithms
in NCCL. Fig. 12 presents the results. For each data size, we repeat
the test 100 times and report the mean and standard deviation.

Related Work
DCN Topology. The Rail-Optimized topology [39] is commonly
used in large-scale clusters but suffers from poor fault tolerance
and high cost. Rail-only reduces cost but still suffers from limited
fault tolerance. HPN [44] addresses the fault tolerance issue with a
dual-ToR structure; however, this design increases network costs
due to the need for additional links. HammingMesh [27] reduces
network costs by designing a topology based on existing deep-
learning traffic patterns. However, changes in model architecture
may introduce more complex traffic, failing to improve all-to-all
performance. SlimFly [6] designs a low-diameter network topology
based on MMS graph theory.
Topology Design Method. While various approaches currently
assist network designers in topology design, large-scale automated
topology design and optimization remain unexplored. Condor [48]
introduces a topology description language for modeling but re-
quires manual design for new topologies. Yijia Chang et al. [10] pro-
pose a unified topology modeling framework. However, these works
still rely on expert experience and do not support performance-
oriented optimization, making them not directly comparable with
ATOP. TopoOpt [52] presents a joint optimization method for topol-
ogy and parallel strategies, but it is limited to small-scale direct-
connect topologies and requires optical switches. Liangyu Zhao et
al. [59] focus on optimizing direct-connect topologies and collective
communication. These works require optical switches and have
limited scalability, whereas ATOP aims to address the problem of
electrical switch-based DCN topology design. Therefore, we do not
compare these approaches to ATOP in the evaluation.
8
Conclusion
In this paper, we propose an automated topology optimization
pipeline, ATOP, capable of identifying the superior large-scale data
center network topology in a short time. ATOP supports the si-
multaneous optimization of multiple objectives, including network
topology performance, hardware cost, and fault tolerance. ATOP
also supports various topology design scenarios, such as building,
optimizing, and expanding data centers. Using ATOP, we discover
ZCube, a new topology with superior cost-effectiveness, low net-
work diameter, and strong fault tolerance. Simulation experiments
show that ZCube improves end-to-end LLM training speed by 3%
to 7% and reduces network hardware costs by 26% to 46% compared
to previous state-of-the-art topologies, including Rail-optimized
Fat-tree (ROFT), Rail-only, and HPN. We also construct ZCube on a
real-world testbed. Results show that ZCube reduces hardware costs
by 25% compared to ROFT while maintaining the same all-reduce
and all-to-all performance.

Convergence and Efficiency of ATOP
In this section, we discuss the convergence and search efficiency of
the NSGA-II algorithm in ATOP.
Convergence. We use three metrics to evaluate convergence, as
shown in Fig. 16. These metrics are the change in the number of
Pareto-optimal topologies as the total number of searched topolo-
gies increases, the Jaccard Distance [12] between Pareto-optimal
sets across generations in NSGA-II, which measures the variation
in the set. In addition, we use HyperVolume [60], a commonly used
indicator in multi-objective optimization tasks, to assess conver-
gence. Fig. 16(a) and (b) show the convergence of ATOP on the
Pareto-optimal set. Fig. 16(c) shows the convergence of ATOP on
the HyperVolume indicator. As the search progresses, the number
of Pareto-optimal topologies stabilizes, with no significant changes,
and the Jaccard Distance decreases and stabilizes, indicating that
the Pareto-optimal set stabilizes in the later stages of the search.
Efficiency. In our configuration, we searched 100,000 topologies
for each GPU scale. For 256 GPUs, the total time for ATOP search
and 2-stage evaluation is 6.5 hours; for 1k GPUs, it is 10.6 hours;
for 4k GPUs, it is 25.4 hours; and for 16k GPUs, it is 71.2 hours.
Most of this time is spent on topology evaluation. These times are
reasonable compared to the several months typically required to
deploy a data center.

Proof of ZCube Diameter
To prove the diameter of ZCube(𝑛,𝑘), we first need to establish the
following lemma:
Lemma 1. In ZCube(𝑛,𝑘+ 1), the longest shortest path from any
level-𝑘switch to any GPU is at most k hops.
Proof: This lemma can be proved using mathematical induction.
For ZCube(𝑛, 1), a single switch connects to 𝑛GPUs, resulting in
a path length of 0 hops. Assume for ZCube(𝑛,𝑘) that the longest
shortest path from any level-(𝑘−1) switch to any GPU is at most
𝑘−1 hops. When constructing ZCube(𝑛,𝑘+1) using 𝑛ZCube(𝑛,𝑘)
and𝑛𝑘switches, each level-(𝑘−1) switch in a ZCube(𝑛,𝑘) connects
to 𝑛different level-𝑘switches, and this process is repeated n times.
Thus, any level-𝑘switch is connected to a level-(𝑘−1) switch in
each ZCube(𝑛,𝑘). By the induction hypothesis, the longest path
from a level-𝑘switch to any GPU is 1 + (𝑘−1) = 𝑘hops.
Next, we prove the network diameter of ZCube(𝑛,𝑘).
Theorem 1. The diameter, defined as the longest shortest path hop
count among all the GPU pairs, of a ZCube(𝑛,𝑘), is k.
Proof: We can construct a path between any pair of GPUs in
ZCube(𝑛,𝑘) as follows: the source GPU sends data through its
𝑘-th port to a level-(𝑘−1) switch. According to Lemma 1, the
longest path from any level-(𝑘−1) switch to any GPU is at most
𝑘−1 hops. By concatenating these two paths, the total path length
is 𝑘hops. Therefore, the path length between any pair of GPUs
does not exceed 𝑘hops.
Theorem 2. The diameter of ZCube(𝑛, 3)-partial is 4.
Proof: By the definition of ZCube(𝑛, 3)-partial, it consists of n
ZCube(𝑛, 2) structures and 𝑛2/2 level-2 switches. If the source and
destination GPUs are within the same ZCube(𝑛, 2), their communi-
cation path length does not exceed 2 hops (Theorem 1). If they are
in different ZCube(𝑛, 2), the path can be constructed as follows: the
source GPU sends data through the second port to a level-1 switch.
The level-1 and level-2 switches are interconnected in a CLOS-like
manner, allowing a one-hop transition to the level-1 switch in the
destination ZCube(𝑛, 2). Since level-1 switches form a complete
bipartite graph with level-0 switches, the path can directly reach
the destination’s level-0 switch. In this construction, the total path
length does not exceed 4 hops.

Fault Tolerance of ZCube
For the fault tolerance of ZCube, we discuss two scenarios: single-
switch failures and link failures. ZCube demonstrates excellent
performance under both types of failures.
Single-switch Failures. ZCube shows robustness against single-
switch failures. When a single switch fails, the GPU connected to
the failed switch can switch to another NIC port.
We present the performance degradation of the 4096 GPUs
ZCube when training the GPT-3 175B model under a single switch
failure in Fig. 3(b) based on our flow-level simulation. The degrada-
tion of ZCube is only 2.8%, while ROFT experiences a 46.9% degra-
dation and HPN experiences a 9.0% degradation, while reducing
32% of the cost compared to HPN.
We also use the 𝐴𝑃𝐿fail introduced in §3.4 to evaluate the fault
tolerance. We compute the average path length between GPU pairs
without switch failures, denoted as𝐴𝑃𝐿. To assess the change before
and after failure, we use the Average Path Stretch (APS), defined
as 𝐴𝑃𝑆= (𝐴𝑃𝐿fail −𝐴𝑃𝐿)/𝐴𝑃𝐿. We choose 𝐴𝑃𝐿fail over 𝐴𝑃𝑆as
the optimization objective in topology searching because 𝐴𝑃𝐿fail
accounts for both performance under single-switch failure and
the topology’s diameter. We consider both scenarios where 8-GPU
servers are interconnected and where no inter-server connections
exist. For the scenario with inter-server connections, we treat the
communication path length between GPU pairs within the same
server as 1, where the 8 GPUs form a full-mesh topology. The results,
shown in Tab. 3, indicate that ZCube(𝑛, 2) achieves the lowest 𝐴𝑃𝐿
and 𝐴𝑃𝐿fail due to its low network diameter and is less affected by
single-switch failures compared to ROFT and Dragonfly topologies.
The HPN topology shows no change in average path length between
GPU pairs before and after failure due to its dual-ToR architecture.
